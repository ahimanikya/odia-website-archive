dmisra · 2001-04-23 09:36:18

<li>Identify the behavior that you want to criticize. Direct your criticism at the action, not the person. The less personal you make your comments, the less likely the person will become defensive and uncooperative.</li> <li>Make criticism specific. Not: "You always miss our schedule updates." But: "You missed the March 15 schedule update." Rash generalizations are counterproductive and tough to prove. The more specific the criticism, the easier it is to justify the criticism.</li> <li>Be sure the behavior you’re criticizing can be changed. Physical characteristics and other similar things cannot always be changed. Pick out specifics the person can work on and see improvement.</li> <li>Use "I" and "we" to stress that you want to work out the problem together, rather than making threats.</li> <li>Make sure the other person understands the reason for your criticism is to improve their performance and themselves. Put a positive spin on your comments; they are meant to improve things.</li> <li>Don’t belabor the point. Short and sweet, no lectures. No one likes to be talked down to.</li> <li>Offer incentives for changed behavior, therefore producing a better work environment. Offer to help the person correct the problem. Improved performance can lead to improved opportunities.</li> <li>Don’t set a tone of anger or sarcasm. Both are counterproductive. The more sarcasm used, the less likely the person will listen.</li> <li>Show the person you understand his or her feelings: “I’ve learned from the same mistakes." Assure the person that we’ve all been there before and had the same feelings or made the same mistakes. Again, the person will not feel alone.</li> <li>If you’re putting your criticism in writing, cool off before writing the critical letter or memo. Be sure only the person it is intended for sees it. If you write your report shortly after an incident, emotions tend to appear.</li> <li>Start off by saying something good. Assure the person that they did some good things and that there are things they can work on to improve.</li> <li>At then end, reaffirm your support and confidence in the person. Reassure the person that you are there to help. You may even tell the person you wouldn’t waste your time with criticism if you didn’t think the person was worth it. End it on a positive note so the person has something to look forward to – and something to work on.</li> Debasmita Misra Senior Engineer (Training & Simulation) Kewaunee Nuclear Power Plant Kewaunee, WI 54115
