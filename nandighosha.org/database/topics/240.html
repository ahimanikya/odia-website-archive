Opening post

dmisra · 2001-08-14 11:40:23

Friends: "<b><font color=brown>Accuracy</font id=brown></b>" and "<b><font color=maroon>Precision</font id=maroon></b>" are often used qualitatively in our daily communication. Most often it is used in lieu of one another. We sometimes say "Precisely" when we mean "Accurately" or vice versa. I was under the notion that both the words could be used independently to mean the same thing until I came across a beautiful piece of communication fron my boss, Mr. Roy Anderson, Chief Nuclear Officer, Nuclear Management Company (NMC) which I am appending below for your perusal and kind information: <b><i><font color=green>To NMC Leadership:</b></i></font id=green> This week I'd like to talk about <b><font color=red>accuracy</b></font id=red> and <b><font color=red>precision</b></font id=red>. Both are important in our daily work and in our communications. But they're different, and sometimes we confuse the two. To understand the difference, let's look at the dictionary. It defines "accuracy" as <b><i><font color=teal>freedom from mistake or error</font id=teal></i></b>. "Precision" is defined as <b><i><font color=teal>the degree of refinement with which an operation is performed or a measurement stated</font id=teal></i></b>. As I said, both are important—but we need to separate the two in our thinking. And we need to focus on accuracy before precision. What does that mean? In our daily work, it means first making sure we're doing the right thing, then doing it with precision. It's possible to do anything with a high degree of refinement, but obviously there's no point in precisely performing an operation that's incorrect in the first place. It would be as if a doctor performed surgery on your arm with great precision, but did so on the wrong arm. (That's a pretty stark example, but it makes the point.) So how do you know what's right? That goes back to using your judgment—bringing your knowledge and experience to bear on the task at hand. I've talked about that before, so I don't need to go into it again here. OK, so we've discussed the importance of separating accuracy and precision in our daily work. What about <b><font color=orange>communications</font id=orange></b>? I think this is where confusion about accuracy and precision most strongly manifests itself. Whether we're addressing an internal or external audience, we need to focus on accuracy first, then precision—if at all. I say "if at all" because sometimes excessive detail can create confusion or mistrust in our listeners' minds, especially among external audiences who don't know all the ins and outs of the nuclear business the way we do. Here's an example. When we discovered that the original shipping bolts had been left in a safety system at the Monticello plant, our most important message to the public was that the plant was safe in spite of that situation. We also informed the public that our safety programs and mechanisms are "in depth"--and that the plant would function properly, even though the bolts had been left inside all these years. Explaining the situation with technical language and terms, such as "operability" or "design basis" may be precise, but it also might mislead or create misunderstanding among your listeners. In other words, the message might be inaccurate overall. How do we communicate with accuracy? We need to follow the basic principles of good communication, especially "<b><font color=red>Target your message to your audience</font id=red></b>." That means looking at things from your listeners' point of view, rather than through your own prism, and speaking in language and terms they can understand. Communicating with accuracy to others also will help you determine the right thing to do and what priority to assign a task, so you'll perform it with the appropriate level of precision. Using the doctor example again, you could spend a great deal of time perfecting the decor in your office—to the detriment of your patients. But that would be a mistake in judgment, and inaccurate in a sense, since accuracy is freedom from mistake or error. Getting back to our industry—please think about the difference between accuracy and precision as you go about your daily work AND when you communicate to others. Don't get caught up in so many details or technicalities that you miss the main objective or focus your energy on the wrong thing. Paraphrasing the old adage: "<b><font color=green>Don't overlook the forest for the trees</font id=green></b>."
