<b><font color=orange>Who or Whom?</font id=orange></b> <b><font color=blue>Question:</font id=blue></b> What are the proper use of the pronouns "who" vs "whom"? <b><font color=blue>Answer:</font id=blue></b> Who relates to whom in the same way he relates to him. Who is the subject, whom is the object. We can keep most pronouns straight most of the time. Native speakers of English, for instance, would never say, "Him go to the party" or "The ball hit he." Yet when using who/whom those same speakers often get confused. The confusion arises because who and whom are used only in questions — "Who called?" "To whom did she speak?" — or in relative clauses: "The boy who lives next door called us." "She spoke to George, with whom she had attended many meetings." <b><font color=blue>Use whom with prepositions:</font id=blue></b> He admired the speaker, for whom he had voted in several elections. ("He had voted for [whom] her in several elections.") To whom did you send this letter? ("You sent the letter to [whom] him.") <b><font color=blue>Use whom when an objective pronoun is clearly needed:</font id=blue></b> Whom did they choose? ("They chose [whom] us.") She was astonished when Rochester, whom she had never seen before, greeted her like a long-lost friend. ("She had never seen [whom] him before.") <b><font color=blue>When in doubt — use who.</font id=blue></b> For further information, look at the pronoun table: <h1>Personal Pronouns:<br>In Case You = Possessive of it, as in "every dog has its day."</strong> </ul><br> <p> <h2><a name="2"></a>Whoever or Whomever?</h2> <p><strong>Question:</strong>In the sentence "I like whomever likes me," what sentence part is the word "whomever"? <p>"Whomever" cannot be a relative pronoun since it is part of a noun clause and not an adjective clause (and therefore is not "relating" anything to the rest of the sentence). It is not part of an adverb clause and therefore cannot be a subordinating conjunction. . . . <p>I . . . have dubbed these words subordinate pronouns, a term I made up. What do you call them? . . . . Been wondering about this for years. <p><strong>Answer:</strong> Beyond any question, pronouns are among the more bothersome parts of speech. <p>As you observe, some grammatical terms are inadequate. In the example you give, "whomever" does not act like a relative pronoun; it does not refer to anything in the main clause. Your invention of "subordinate pronoun" makes sense. Unless they are used in questions, relative pronouns always introduce dependent clauses. <p>But another issue arises in your example: the question of "whomever/whoever." "Who/whom" distinctions can befuddle the brightest. The example you quote, "I like whomever likes me, " illustrates one of the many problems this distinction creates. <p><strong>Let us break down the sentence:</strong> <p><strong>I</strong> [subject] <strong>like</strong> [transitive verb] <strong>whomever likes me</strong> [a dependent clause functioning as the direct object] <p>Now, one would suppose that "whomever" is the right pronoun to use. It appears, after all, in a clause that is the object of the main verb. <p>However, when we break down the dependent clause, we see that the pronoun needs to be in the subjective case: <p><em><strong>whoever </strong>[subject] <strong>likes</strong> [transitive verb] <strong>me</strong> [direct object]</em> <p><em>How the pronoun functions in its <strong>own </strong>clause determines its case:</em> <ul>[*]If the pronoun is the <strong>subject</strong> of its own clause, it takes the subjective case (who, whoever). [*]If the pronoun is the <strong>object</strong> in its own clause, it takes the objective case (whom, whomever). </ul> <p> How the pronoun's clause functions in the sentence doesn't matter. <p>Many word mavens believe that "whom" is disappearing from the language and that we shall be quite rid of it in a matter of decades. Some hope that is the case. Edited by - dmisra on July 02 2001 1:19:52 PM
