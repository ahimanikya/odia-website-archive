From Roy Anderson (Chief Nuclear Officer, Nuclear Management Company): Can anyone tell me the difference between information, knowledge, wisdom and leadership? So far I've received more than 40 responses, and they're still coming. Even more impressive than the number of responses was their quality— they all showed understanding and depth of thought, and many of them were quite eloquent. Now, here's my crack at my own question: <b><font color=red>INFORMATION</font id=red></b> is simply data—raw material. It's like a news report that you listen to and passively absorb, but can't do anything about. <b><font color=red>KNOWLEDGE</font id=red></b> is an understanding of information and requires work on your part. Knowledge places information in a broader context of things like history, processes, people, comparisons of data, and so on. Acquiring knowledge takes effort; you have to dig deeper into information to turn it into knowledge. <b><font color=red>WISDOM</font id=red></b> goes one step further— wisdom encompasses knowledge and what you do with your knowledge. One twist on wisdom, though, is that wisdom doesn't get anything done unless you choose to translate it into action. Wisdom requires personal effort to obtain but it involves no personal risk. Think of one of those political talk shows where the guest seems to know it all – there is a lot of wisdom being brought forward but very little risk because there is no action. Finally, <b><font color=red>LEADERSHIP</font id=red></b> is the application of wisdom for the purpose of achieving a goal. Leadership also involves change—as one responder to my question said, "Leadership is the act of influencing, in a wise, insightful manner, those around you to change—or make changes for the better." Leadership requires personal risk and involvement because it requires action; a leader is a doer, not an observer. Leadership is also situational and requires all of us to recognize who is the best qualified person to deal with a situation. That person must step up and lead, and we must respect and support that person in his/her leadership role. A classic example is CPR and a heart attack victim. In a heart attack situation, the EMT or the person who knows CPR is the leader, not necessarily the highest ranking manager on the scene. This example demonstrates one of the difficulties organizational or appointed leaders have, which is to recognize that in some situations they are not the natural leader and that their job is to support the situational leader. Leadership involves "followership" in certain situations. So, I respectfully ask you to avoid falling into the trap of thinking you have the corner on knowledge and wisdom in every situation, just because you're an appointed leader. Let employees exercise their judgment, their leadership, when the situation warrants. And, as I've said before, give employees the resources and wherewithal to do their jobs. It takes courage to be a natural leader, but I'm confident we are capable of summoning up that courage. Recognize when it's your time to lead, and do what needs to be done. +++++++++++++++++++++++++++++++++++++++++++++++++++++++++++ You do not have to read the following if not interested. It is long. This means taking your past experiences and recommending actions to benefit an entity/person/company. After gathering information, retaining it for future use, and overlying past experience with the knowledge, you display leadership.
