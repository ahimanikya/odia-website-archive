The Housing Situation in India

  When we consult the relevant statistics, we see that there are more than 14 crore houses in India, distributed among 25 large cities, 2500 small and medium towns, and over 500,000 villages. The number of homeless people in India is 18 crores, and hence the number of additional homes needed is at least 3 crores. The average time and the cost consumed per house built over the last 24 years have been 100 seconds and Rs. 33,000 respectively. Thus, to satisfy the housing needs of everybody, we would need another 1000 years and Rs. 99,000 crores. This situation is truly alarming.

Construction costs have been increasing at over 15% each year; houses built with conventional materials cost around Rs. 200--250 per sqft, making even a 200 sqft house well beyond the reach of the poor. In addition, the eighth five-year plan forecasts shortages of 17 million tons of cement, 3 million tons of steel, over 50,000 million bricks etc. Since conventional building materials are both extremely expensive and in short supply, there is a dire need to look beyond traditional building/construction methods. Increased and quick supply of housing is possible only by using alternative, appropriate, efficient and cost-effective techniques.

Mobility is high among the rural poor due to seasonal employment and the lack of a permanent base. They have no opportunity to get involved in any training or literacy programmes, and have to continuously struggle for subsistence. Most do not have any specialised skills that could help them out of a life of dependence. Permanent low-cost housing, combined with skills training, will provide these people with a root: they will then be able to motivate themselves and to establish themselves in more permanent employment, and their children will attend schools more regularly. Also, any money spent on building houses is an investment that stays in the village, increasing the local wealth. Villagers get employment in building their own houses, and acquire new skills of masonry, carpentry, etc. The flow of talent and young people to urban areas is also stemmed. Experimental/innovative designs for low-cost housing provide valuable educational experience. Besides providing permanent shelter, SVT believes that building houses will allow the villagers to develop a long term vision into the future, taking them beyond their usual year--to--year outlook. Thus SVT is first trying to address the three basic human needs --- food, clothing and shelter.
