Anatomy of a famine and our role therein

Priyadarsan Patra (Darshan)

What is common about Sub-saharan Sahel region, Ethiopia, Somalia, Sudan, Kalahandi-Bolangir-Malkangiri, South Bihar, Bangladesh,... ? The ancient scourge of famine is indeed a global phenomenon, threatening to engulf more of it if left unchallenged at its roots. For most of us in affluent countries, it is an easy matter to perfunctorily attribute various causes to this extreme form of human and environmental deprivation --- almost as convenient as hearing about a traffic death in local news, ranting against that `irresponsible Corvette driver' and then completely wiping it out from the memory, come next morning! Or at best, we donate a few dollars to a (usually high overhead) charity expecting them to ship large quantities of our surplus food, not even worrying if the target region has any roads, or docks to receive or if it degenerates into greasing off the very palms that blessed the famine in the first place, or, if a massive food import is even helpful.

George Lucas of Clemson University in the ``Journal of Philosophy''(Dec, 1990) submits that up until 1970, famine was treated as a temporally and spatially specific natural disaster brought about by a calamity like an earthquake, a flood or a prolonged drought. So the instinctive response of the world at large was to rush in large amounts of food and medicine aid until the emergency relents. But, the recurring crisis situations of 1970s as in Bangladesh & Ethiopia made this ``donation strategy'' give way to two schools of thought --- both convinced on need for a longterm solution, but sharply differing on their views of the causes and hence proposed remedies. The first school called the Developmentalists see sudden drop in food supplies as the primary cause and advise drastic overhaul and modernization of the farming and irrigation structure, cash-cropping, and use of latest agricultural techniques such as `miracle' seeds developed by Norman Bourlag, ``father of Green Revolution''. On the other hand, Neo-Malthusians or Environmentalists argue that it is not the inadequate availability of food and natural resources like water, energy, land & fertilizer, but the excessive demand on the ``carrying capacity'' of a region and environmentally destructive agro-practices (e.g., overgrazing in Sahel, timber-cutting in Nepal), mainly brought on by over-population, that instigate famine. They have even at times propounded letting the victims to simply starve by denying aid until a ``natural ecological balance'' is restored, according to Lucas. Lucas, drawing upon ideas of economist Amartya Sen, refutes both these schools of thought by citing examples where famine has occurred in years when food supply in the target country is stable (sometimes even exported) and adequate to feed the existing population, whereas sudden population growths and environmental degradation have not necessarily created famine. He argues that famines are microeconomic disasters largely functional on local political and economic policies: unemployment patterns, taxation, inflationary pressures (particularly on food prices), governmental pressure for `development programs' like cash-cropping, absence of minimal employment or welfare relief, and border disputes between emerging nations inhibiting traditional nomadic migration, for instance. He suggests that Redcross and other charity organizations world-wide should set up some kind of ``food stamp'' programs and lend money to boost purchasing power of the locals, rather than engage in the wasteful worldwide procurement and shipping of food. Additionally, excessive food imports can depress the food prices for local farmers and retailers driving them out of business. He seems to emphasize distribution as a key player, perhaps beside the other two, `demand' and `supply' which we have mentioned before.

Kalahandi-Bolangir-Koraput Famine
In the backdrop of an often acrimonious debate among these three threads of thought, a very real famine has been brewing thick again in the tribal dominated Kalahandi-Bolangir(KB) region of Orissa and in Southern Bihar. India Abroad (June 25, '93; Canadian issue) reports some 11 million people are affected, severely in about 600 villages. 500 have starved to death. The governmental aid is all tangled up in bureaucratic bungling and corruption (see India Today, May 31, 1993). The famine in KB area has been recurring since 1967 in recent memory. India Today (February 15, 93) reports: ``If Kalahandi plumbed the lowest depths of human misery, it pales in comparison with the contiguous famine-ridden district of Bolangir in western Orissa. Rukh Meher, 60, of Bhalamunda, a nondescript village of about 50 huts of mainly unemployed farm laborers is willing to sell you her three grandchildren. She offers her eldest grandchild--for Rs. 1,000. Getting no response, she slashes the price to Rs. 500 before finally coming down to Rs. 100, virtually begging at this point: `Please take her, she is a nice child. If you don't take her, she will starve to death with me'... ''

Lalu Mansinha, a professor at the University of Western Ontario, in an article for Orissa Society of Americas Journal(1993) wrote, ``Imagine a man-made debacle in which One Million Three Hundred Thousand People died. Imagine one out of every three person that you know die a horrible lingering undignified death. That nightmare happened in Orissa in 1866. The world does not remember the worst famine in India's recorded history, probably the worst famine ever, anywhere. In India the event is barely mentioned in history books. The horror of it all is that it was preventable... The last famine in Europe, the Irish Potato Famine of 1846 resulted in some deaths, but nothing on the scale of Orissa 20 years later. In India, Bengal suffered the same crop failures as Orissa in 1865. With a diversified economy, and much better transportation, the reported deaths were about 45,000.... We learn from history that we do not learn from history. There have been persistent press reports of starvation in Kalahandi and neighbouring districts and attempts to sell or give away children....'' Many have correctly observed that collapse of purchasing power of the locals accelerates famine situation, e.g., the adjoining district of Sambalpur is very fertile, irrigated and abundant in crops. Many urban areas produce no food, yet they are not famine-stricken. The central and state governments aided by some foreign lending and aid institutions have attempted a few huge development programs, which have run aground due to inter-departmental rivalry, sloth, cost-overruns, and poor planning. For example, the hilly areas near the Upper Indravati project area of Koraput & Kalahandi receive 150 inches of yearly rain, but due to denuded forests, millions of tons of soil is washed away muddying water streams of as far as Nawarangpur. The World Bank and the Indian central government had slated funds for afforestation in 2,630 sq. km. of area. As of March, '93 the work remained undone due to interdepartmental rivalry and misplanning, reports Times of India (Mar 18, '93). In other cases, implemented `development' programs were not really for the locals' development or benefit: e.g., the irrigation-cum-resettlement scheme of the 1960s involved resettlement of displaced families from erstwhile East Pakistan and implementation of the Potteru project to irrigate 48,850 hectares in Rabi season and 61,034 hectares in Kharif. In the name of rehabilitation, 40,000 acres of forest cover was cleared, but Potteru supposed to have finished by 1977, has barely started and now deadlocked over cost-estimates for several years. For an example of dismal lack of communication facilities and resultant paralyzing isolation, consider the 165 tribal villages of Kudumuluguma block and 93 villages in Mahili which are completely cut off during the rain. Scarcity of water here and total absence of water-related infrastructure there conspire with prolonged political neglect and lack of purchasing power making common life unbearable in the region. Echoing Gandhi's ideas of village economy and sarvodaya, E.F.Schumacher, in his celebrated book Small is Beautiful, declares ``Unless life in the hinterland can be made tolerable, the problem of world poverty is insoluble and will inevitably get worse.''

What is our role?
Since March, 1993 there has been a lot of discussion and soul-searching on an electronic network of Orissans (including the author), called ORNET, on ways for Non-resident Indians to help the economically backward Indian state of Orissa. The shocking news of starvation and destitution in the KB area helped congeal the energy of a small group of people and the author suggested forming a group to rally up people and resources around the cause of grass-roots development in the region. The author discussed this idea with India Progressive Action Group(IPAG) of Austin, a forward-looking group he has been with since its inception and IPAG has lent its organizational support. A few individuals have come forward to lead the so-called ``Kalahandi-Bolangir Initiative'' which has now grown to more than 60 ardent supporters over several regions in the USA, Canada and at various university campuses. Meanwhile, the central government of India has agreed to grant about $26 million as relief to the entire region in Orissa. It's probably too little and too late. Yet, whatever small percentage of this relief survives to trickle down to the mass runs the all-too-common danger of being used thoughtlessly thereby only perpetuating the misery. With this and a perceived moral responsibility of ours in view, we wish to contribute towards promotion of grass-roots involvement and sustainable development in that part of India. We hope this feeling will be crystalized by local volunteers and development workers --- complementing any good effort by the government.

We are delighted indeed that our effort is growing into a movement of sorts, among some sympathetic non-resident Indians, for a `participatory consciousness' about sustainable and equitable development, particularly in Orissa and generally in India. In this regard, we are seeking and raising modest contributions and are also in touch with about half-a-dozen reputed volunteer groups(Non-Governmental Organizations) in Orissa, some development experts throughout India and a few progressive sister organizations in the USA. We have been soliciting and reviewing project proposals that have reliable objectives of longterm and midterm solutions to the local socio-economic problem of development in small areas within the famine-ravaged region. We are seeking active participation of you for reach-out, for research on specific agro techniques and, for review of proposals and subsequent monitoring of projects, to volunteer time in future at a project site, or for educated discussion on alternate development methods suitable for Third World, etc.
