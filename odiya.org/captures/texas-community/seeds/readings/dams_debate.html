Summary of a review of the book "The Big Dams: Claims and Counter Claims,
B.D. Dhawan (ed), Common Wealth Publishers, New Delhi, Rs. 220" by
R. Maria Saleth, Economic and Political Weekly, July 25, 1992, PP 1607-9.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
The book under review contains contributions made by both the proponents
and opponents of big dams from various professional backgrounds - academicians,
journalists, activists, engineers, and administrators.

The first article 'Mounting Antagonism towards Big Dams' is by Dhawan who
argues for big dams. He says that forest loss due to big dams is small
when compared to loss due to illegal felling and forest encroachment.
While ridiculing the small-dams option, he suggests an appropriate scaling-
down of the dam height to account for ecological and equity issues.

The next chapter is a criticism of Dhawan's article by Ashok K. Mitra, 
Chandrasekhar Pant, Shripad Dharmadhikary, and Nirmal Sengupta, followed
by Dhawan's rejoinder. Mitra focuses on the benefit-cost ratio (BCR)
methodology and wonders if the BCR criteria favors big dams if cost
factors for ecosystems loss, dam safety, and land loss due to waterlogging
and salinity are included. He also suggests weightages for various cost-benefit
parameters. Pant and Dharmadhikary talk about govt's reluctance to
engage in public debates and why the opposition to big dams is actually
about the very process of faulty development. Sengupta argues that Dhawan's
medium-dams option, if taken to its logical extreme, reduces to the
small-dams option. Dhawan's rejoinder is that he is trying to find a
middle ground between dam storage and land submergence. He also says that
one can't quantify non-market goods such as ecosystem loss, genetic 
diversity, social equity etc.

Arun Ghosh in 'A Plea for Open Govt: The Case of Narmada Project' talks
about the need for a public dialogue and denounces the government for 
the suppression of basic information related to the project,
(mis)use of legislations like the Official Secrets Act, etc.

Ramasamy R Iyer in 'Large Dams: The Right Perspective' talks about the
much-overlooked socio-political aspects of big dams which reinforce
the existing land inequities and create secondary displacement of
poor peasants. Iyer also suggests a radical change in the BCR methodology.
Jasveen Jairath criticizes the prevailing development strategies
which are divorced from the requirements of the masses and says that
dam height reduction, focus on BCR methodology etc, are 
attempts to technicalize an essentially social
and political issue. Iyer in his rejoinder says that envtlists should
offer viable alternatives with a detailed operational plan instead of
condemning exiting plans. [The reviewer notes here that a detailed
alternative plan need not be a precondition to recognize the limitations
of a current plan.]

R.B. Shah, in 'Role of Major Dams in Indian Economy'  presents a strong
argument in favor of large dams and citing a study conducted in the
Jonk sub-basin of the Mahanandi delta, states that construction of smaller
dams will result in more forest loss from land submergence than the case
with a large dam.

In his second paper titled 'Major and Minor Irrigation Works: Cost Aspects
of the Controversy', Dhawan concludes that if the investment made on the
private account, operating costs, and supporting investment like
electricity generation, transmission, and distribution network are
included in minor irrigation schemes, the cost advantage will decisively
favor major irrigation by a margin of 3:1. Ashok Mitra counters that
interest costs on investment during the gestation period are not
included for major irrigation projects and that there is a double
counting caused by including electricity costs both in operational
and capital costs. Dhawan replies that his calculations are not free
from error and speculation.

Manu Shroff, in 'Point, Counterpoint', answers all critics of big dams.
He claims that the Narmada Valley Project is economically justified
since its internal rate of return of 12 % is close to the opportunity
cost of capital. 

Rusi Engineer, in 'The Sardar Sarovar Controversy: Are the Critics Right?',
focuses on rehabilitation and envtl impact of big dams.

K.C. Thomas, in 'Water Resources of India and Its Development Prospects',
defends big dams since hydro-electric power generation is better and
environmentally safer than coal or gas-based power generation. He questions
the technical and economical viability of small dams and the idea of
dam height reduction. 

Kanchan Chopra in her article suggests modifications to the BCR technique to
properly account for the differential impact of a project on different
socio-economic groups, environmetal impact, etc.

                            WHY I WILL DROWN

Patrick McCully talks to Medha Patkar, internationally-renowned activist 
from India's Save the Narmada Movement -- the Narmada Bachao Andolan 
(NBA) -- which is fighting to stop the massive Sardar Sarovar dam and 
irrigation project. Despite the recent cancellation of a major World Bank 
loan, the Gujarat state government remains commited to the project.

"We will cling to the land like a baby clings to its mother. When the 
waters rise in the monsoon starting this June we will face them as we 
have always vowed to do. The Samarpit Dal -- the Save or Drown Squad 
-- of commited activists will not allow any land in the valley to be 
submerged without sacrifice of human life. It is not suicide and we do 
not want to die but our commitment to face the waters has been the 
bedrock of our movement. Our decision to be submerged with the land is 
the logical consequence of our long struggle to save the Narmada and to 
force a rethinking of the direction of development in India and around the 
world. Our sacrifice will be for life not death -- for the life of the tribal 
people and farmers in the rest of the valley and all those threatened by 
big dams and the other symptoms of anti-people development.

The sacrifice of the Samarpit Dal will be supported by thousa from the valley and all over India who will gather for the monsoon 
in Manibeli, the second village behind the dam. Manibeli has become a 
national symbol of resistance. Over the last few years the tribal people 
in the village have been arrested and beaten and subjected to all sorts of 
psychological pressure to make them move but they remain strong. In 
February we started our 'People's Verdict' in Manibeli. We went from 
house to house throughout the entire 200km-long submergence zone 
asking families if they were prepared to oppose the dam by refusing to 
move; now 22,523 homes in the valley -- around three-quarters of those 
to be submerged -- display a metal plaque saying 'We will drown but not 
move'. 

This year the dam wall is over a third complete and large parts of 
Manibeli and several other villages will be submerged for many months. 
Scores of families will be affected. If there is a very heavy monsoon 
nearly 30 villages will go under water and many hundreds if not 
thousands of families will have their homes and their crops washed 
away. If they escape drowning they will face hunger.

Many of these families are resolutely opposing the dam and will not 
move. Others, afraid of what will become of them, are prepared to move 
but have not yet been resettled. Increasing numbers of families are 
returning from their resettlement sites, prefering the uncertainty of 
impending submergence to the lack of land, water, fuel, fodder and other 
necessities they face when they move. 

Repression is increasing in the valley as submergence approaches. Last 
week some villagers were arrested just because they had returned to 
their village from a resettlement site and decided to resist the dam with 
the NBA. A tribal woman who is one of our strong activists was badly 
beaten in custody. The people will not permit this repression to go on. We 
are fighting forced evictions in the courts and now human rights 
organisations across India have decided to form a committee to monitor 
the situation in the valley. International human rights groups will also be 
watching what happens. 

The exit of the World Bank from Sardar Sarovar has given a great boost 
to the NBA and our supporters and shown that the dam is not a fait 
accompli. Since their withdrawal there has been a huge change in the 
opinion of politicians and the electorate in Gujarat state who are now 
seriously questioning government propaganda about the costs and 
benefits of the dam. There is no doubt that Gujarat does not have the 
funds to finish the dam and it looks increasingly unlikely that the central 
government will provide the money. [In fact the central government 
announced on 22 April that it will put $170m into the project -- the sum 
outstanding on the World Bank's cancelled loan. However, the multi-
billion dollar project remains in deep financial difficulties] The question 
now is not whether the project will be completed but how much damage 
will be done before it grinds to a halt 

The World Bank still has responsiblity for any deaths and repression that 
occur as it has supported the project until now. The last thing we want 
is any more money from the Bank -- or any other foreign source -- we 
want an apology from Bank President Lewis Preston and we want a 
statement that they will fund no more projects in the Narmada valley. 

The British Overseas Development Administration is the only foreign 
funder still involved in Sardar Sarovar. They are funding environmental 
and hydrological studies with no attempt to consult with the affected 
people. They will not even tell us if the studies will be made public, 
saying this depends on the results. Just like the World Bank, they will 
only release information if it suits them. Luckily we have experience of 
dealing with British imperialism in India. We will throw the ODA out just 
as we threw out their colonialist predecessors.

We do not demand that the project be scrapped, but that construction be 
stopped while it is comprehensively and publicly reviewed with the full 
involvement of the NBA, the affected people and independent specialists. 
All the alternatives must be considered. This is the only thing that can 
stop our sacrifice."

Funding for the Narmada Valley Project has been stopped following the Govt.
of India's withdrawal of request for a loan of $450 million. The Govt. chose 
to do this following a long history of events. In April '87, the Rajiv 
Gandhi Govt. decided to start construction for a project whose foundation
stone was laid by his grandfather. Various environmental organizations arose
like the Narmada Bachao Andolan who have attempted to stop the project. Parts
of the Indian press, particularly the Illustrated Weekly of India, have openly
opposed the project. The World Bank therefore set up an investigation into
the matter; a Bradford Morse and a Thomas Berger suggested that there was not
enough information to size up the ecological consequences and that the 
relocation of the 30,000 families living in the valley was unjust. The World
Bank set extremely stringent time bound parameters for resettlement which
the Indian Govt. was not able to meet while upholding proper democratic
principles and was forced to withdraw its request inorder to save face. The
Japanese have also withdrawn aid.

	Let me first provide some of the standard arguements offered by the
environmental community:
(1) It is very expensive, almost $ 3 Billion
(2) It will not produce as much electricity as the Govt. claims
(3) The relocation of the families by a 100 Km. is not acceptable unless more
land is provided to those displaced
(4) The land in the Kutch area which is expected to be cultivated due to 
irrigation would salinize rapidly.
(5) the 150 meter wall, the reservoir, 100,000 Km. of canals will end in
some sort of an ecological disaster
	As counter arguements, there are the following:
(1) At independence, a few million were relocated and 10 million fled in the
70's from Bangladesh(atleast 30,000 have arrived in Guantanamo Bay from Haiti)
(2) The regions of the Punjab and Haryana irrigated by the Bhakra Nangal 
produces about 40% of the country's grain. 
(3) The electricity generated will increase the nations capacity by 1% through
the use of a renewable non-explosive source
(4) Environmental doomdayers are usually wrong.

	All these intellectual arguements while interesting do not still 
suggest the right course of action. There is however one more compelling
reason. Of the 25 million children born in India each year, 4 million never
make the age it to the age of 6, a large proportion of whom die of water
borne diseases like diarrhoea and dysentary and cholera. In two years, this
is more people than the number of Jews who died in WWII. The Narmada Valley
project is expected to bring drinking water to 4% of the Indian population
or about 30 million people.

Indian Govt. asks WB to pull out of Narmada

In a vote at the World Bank last October a decision was taken to provide
the Indian Government with an additional 6 months to meet all the
resettlement and rehabilitation standards of the World Bank. Now, at
the end of those 6  months, the Indian government requests that further
World Bank loans for the Sardar Sarovar project be cancelled and pledges
to finance the project, to the tune of an extra $170 million.

Why is it that the Indian Government no longer wants World Bank money?
Why is it that it did not wait for the World Bank executive directors to
meet and decide if they want to continue financing the project or not?
The answers are easy to guess.  First of all, it was evident that the
World Bank standards for resettlement and rehabilitation would not be
met in 6 months. The only way the WB could have continued support would
have been by turning a blind eye to the socio-economic and technical
shortcomings of the project, and the international pressure would have
precluded this. Thus, the World Bank would have withdrawn anyway, but
if it had come to that both parties would have had to admit to the
flaws not only in the project but in their whole policy of
non-participatory development, where the people most affected never got
a say in the policy.

Secondly, as long as the World Bank was associated with it
the project had to be a development project. Therefore the promoters had
to resort to a plethora of false claims about benefits of the project
to justify its enormous environmental and social costs. Now within the
current political climate in India, where might is right, they have
almost a carte blanche over the issue and they no longer have to bother
about meeting international standards of resettlement and rehabilitation.

How does the Gujrat government plan to get that much money to finance
the project? The required amount to finish the project is more than its
whole outlay for irrigation projects through the 5 year plan, and
it already has a staggering budgetary deficit of the order of Rs 6
billion. Obviously it has to get the money from elsewhere.  It has
already resorted to desperate tactics by distributing
the money obtained from the Centre for riot-victims, in the form of
bonds to the project. The possibility of channeling money from other WB
loans cannot be ruled out. But an interesting thing that must be mentioned
here is the fact that a major chemical and fertilizer plant
set up has been planned out for the Bharuch district of Gujrat, close
to the dam site. These units are going to be financed by the private
sector and might also include some NRI investment. These would of course
require power and water, and this could be a clue as to how
the government intends to finance the dam. To be explicit,
there are rumours that the Gujrat Govt., instead of seeking
to supply water to the masses for irrigation and domestic purposes,
now intends to turn this project into a more profitable
power-generating venture.

It would be naive to believe that the Gujrat Govt. is serious about solving
the water problems of the state.  For over seven years now,
it has been trying to implement this multi-million dollar project
despite severe criticism from civil engineers, scientists, anthropologists
and environmentalists.  In the meantime, Gujrat reels from severe
droughts in the summer. Kutch, Saurashtra and other areas remain dry
throughout the year.  Indeed,
in just the last two decades the number of villages that have
been deprived of their water-source has gone up from 4,000 to 12,000.
For how long are the villagers going to suffer?

The first priority of the Gujrat Govt. should be to ensure that
water for drinking, other domestic purposes and for irrigation is made
available to the people.  And it can achieve this by
implementing, other less costly but more appropriate methods such as
harvesting the rainwater and recharging the groundwater. The state of
Gujrat receives 105 MAF (million acre feet) of rainfall every year and
even 20% of this, if harvested, is more than twice the amount that
authorities claim is the potential of this project. In this
context, it also needs to be pointed out that the present problems of
Saurashtra and Kutch, the increase in salinity due to the ingress of
the sea and the almost total depletion of fresh groundwater, have been
directly caused by the so-called "Green Revolution" of the sixties.
Then too, despite protests from environmentalists, the Gujrat Govt.
launched on a massive programme to install tube-wells and pump-sets
with the result that over-pumping caused the water table to go down.
This project too will have damaging long term ramifications on the people
and the whole eco-system of the Narmada. These have not been debated and
understood by the project authorities, and the way this project has been
designed and executed to date seems to have been motivated mainly by myopic
political gains.

Recent studies indicate that capital-intensive, Western technology is
not always appropriate for overpopulated third-world countries like
India.  The Narmada project, like many other developmental projects
throughout India, is merely going to siphon off money from the state
budget, increase the foreign debt of the central budget, and in the
bargain line the pockets of private industrialists and the like. Very
little, if anything at all will remain for the villager who is ousted
from his home and the peasant who suffers due to the lack of water.
It is high time that the people of India protested against these
unfair methods of development.



S. V. Jagannath

There seem to be two kinds of activism - one which works with 
the government at improving the system, and one which denies
the whole system - and feels that such a system can never
be "improved upon". Long back, in regard to the first, someone 
told me - "Jooth par jooth nahin khada kar sakte ho.". { you
cannot keep stacking lies, one on another. } It has taken me 
some years to begin to vaguely understand what he meant.

And then again, how honest is one ready to get? If one believes
that the system is wrong, how ready is one to opt out of it? 

And how ready will we be to pay the real cost of things ? What is 
the cost of the electricity which drives this mode of communication 
for instance ? What cost can be placed on the destruction of entire 
families  hounded out of their existance ? And if that cost can be 
calculated, again, is he who can afford it entitled to buy it ? Or is 
he, who is hounded out, entitled to question ? And if he is told that it 
is for "national good", may he ask for proof of that ? Who is answerable 
to him ? Today the same tribal who was driven out yesterday when the Bargi 
dam was built higher up on Narmada, questioned at the meeting at
Manibeli - " this forest and the river, I have used since time 
immemorial. Was Narmada made in Delhi/ Bombay that the government 
orders who can use it ?" the Bargi oustees are joining their tribal 
bretheren today to fight against the Sardar Sarovar project. 

But the woman from Bargi who came to join the padyatra { a walk
across many miles, and lasting days. a method to protest, and 
show solidarity, and build awareness. } lasting thro' this month 
also said, " In our scriptures it is written that the Narmada can 
never be tied down by man. So we never struggled. Now she has been 
tied down ... ;  This is written in the vedas." The andolan { andolan
means revolt. the protest is spearheaded by NBA - narmada bachao
andolan - translated as 'revolt to save narmada' - a people's 
movement across much of the affected area. } has made a phenomenal 
differance. Just a handful of people built up this momentum ... 
The poorest want to fight for their rights. But they are sometimes 
too intimidated. Sometimes a slight outside support helps. Someone 
who can tell them their rights. Who can tell them of other struggles.

This struggle seems to be more than just against the dam - it is
against a system divorced from people. And unanswerable to those
violated against. The police atrocities are reminiscent of the british 
oppression. the govt/"system"/powers that be decide that the 
"revolt" has to be broken "at any cost". this message gets passed 
down and the result is lathis (police beatings) and every form of 
intimidation. the temper of the villagers is rising today. and it 
is taking effort on the part of the andolan to tell them that the 
slightest violence is all the government is waiting for to get an
excuse to destroy the whole effort. all these stories reminded
me of the stories of our struggle for independance. at koliari, one 
of the villagers, an old man who had lost 23 acres of land for a 
resettlement due of 2 acres said, " my son is a teacher and only
because of that i am able to survive today. let the government 
official come to my village, and i will cut off his hands." the 
anger and anguish is palpable.

manhandling of activists is common. the police sometimes come up with 
- ' see, we understand that what you are doing is right. but we have 
our orders to break the andolan at any cost. see, we have children ...'. 
and then if someone replies with, 'so ? can't you see this tribal child ? 
don't you know what you are doing to his life ?', its the lathi. but 
then can any of us take being reminded of the true extent of the honesty 
of our values ?

the police intimidation in very bad. the people involved in this
have seen many other factes and stories now. like, a woman who was 
jailed for stealing a saree, and tho' the penalty would have been 
only 6 days of imprisonment, had been kept there for over 6 months as 
she did not have 2000/- for bail. of many people who have been pulled 
into jail as the police has to show his "quota". and once released, with 
a police record, they get no other job and move into a life of crime. of 
undertrials who do not know of their rights. of how it is entirely the 
lower class in these lockups, for the same reasons of ignorance and fear. 
and of course, more petty crimes.

today the villager is losing the fear of the police and government 
official. ' what can they do ? beat us up ? imprison us ? we have 
tasted the worst, so let them do what they want to.' and with the 
loss of that awe, the struggle can only become healthier and stronger.

i was at baroda/ manibeli / vadgam for a week. at the base, where the
truth is, there are only very few people committed to helping the
villagers put their point across/ help them in asking for justice. this
handful of 7/8 people of the NBA handle everything from cooking to faxing, 
to handling village meetings, to handling visitors to the place, to 
going with the villagers to place their law suits and to help 
them in the cases. well, often there is only little time to spare 
for cooking, and life goes on on endless cups of tea. upma is a 
standard fast food ! a lot of time is spent in travelling. at 
minutes' notice, one leaves for the bus stop with a jhola
(bag), for a bus to the nearest point to the village and a trek. so much 
of their time is taken up in trying to reach/ to communicate with others.
and there the two universes that exist in our country stand out. the 
e-mail world where there is communication with U.S. in minutes, and 
this world where you need to walk miles to reach a phone to reach out to 
others. and a lot of time is spent in commuting. the andolan has two 
heavy motorcycles, which are used for priority commute, like in cases 
when someone has to reach manibeli soon. in such cases these people 
take the bus to kevadia colony ( the colony for the dam engineers.) and 
then are motorcycled down to near manibeli - a 45 minute ride. then
the narmada is crossed on a small boat rowed by one of the tribals. 
another NGO, as it was getting wound up, gave their jeep to the andolan, 
and now they have that too.  

the composition of these people is 3 engineers, 2 from a social service 
background, two jounalists - one of whom came to cover some meeting, 
and then wrote back to his editor that he was staying on here. the 
people are in their twenties and early thirties. and then a lot of the 
activists are from the villages - they are into the andolan on a full 
time basis. there is no bias against woman leadership - and the behns 
(sisters) are often stauncher and stronger. as far as medha's work goes, 
she is generally everywhere. one of the villagers from nimad told me - 
" hum tho kahthe hain ke woh narmada mai he hain. woh na hothe tho, 
hum kab ka bhikar jaathe."  { we say that she is narmada incarnate 
herself. if she were not there, we would have got scattered long ago. } 
at the meeting in manibeli, one villager bent to touch her feet and 
she jerked away her feet - " aise kabhi math karo. haath doosre ke paun 
ko choone ke liye nahin. utake ladne ke liye hotha hai." { never do that. 
hands are for raising and fighting with for one's rights. not for 
touching another's feet with. ever. }

basically it ends up in too few people handling too many things.
the handful at the base are very very pressed for time. medha 
said that so many villages had not been reached at all because 
of this. and that if they had more people/ more time a lot more 
could be done. today awareness is building up in gujarat too - the 
canal affected villages. and the kutch/ saurashtra people are realizing 
the actual extent of the purported benefits.

when i was there, carol had also come as part of an australian
NGO which was covering this. she's spent long times in india,
and told me very simply - " i've always loved india. i always come
here when i can. " felt so nice to hear that. she was quite cool in 
a salwar kameez, sipping chai { tea}  out of cracked cups, sleeping on 
the floor. travelling standing on the same moffusil buses. 

manibeli is the first village in maharashtra marked for submergence.
the first house to be submerged as such is in vadgam in gujarat.
these two villages face each other across the narmada. these are all 
tribal villages. at manibeli they have built a couple of huts for the 
andolan which function as the office there. and there are baba and 
sukhdev there from MP villages who are part of the andolan. baba is an 
old man who cooks and makes endless cups of tea for everyone. 'kada chai' 
- no milk, and 'gur' { unrefined sugar } instead of sugar. anyway, its 
a good stimulant, tho'. when i was talking to baba about how poor the 
villagers may actually be, and how that may compare with the slum life, 
he said that they had been to cities and seen 'jhopad pattiyan'{slums}, 
and were much better off farming their pieces of land. that they did 
not want to be richshaw pullers in cities then bela asked him, 'baba, 
do you believe in god ?'. he said, 'of course.'. bela - ' then why does 
he not listen to us ?' baba - ' who knows ? we must have done wrong 
sometime.'

these tribal villages are very different. a village could extend to 
many miles - there are vast spaces and forests between huts. and in 
the words of a village behn in manibeli itself - ' whatever we may 
lack, we don't lack food. in times of low monsoon, the forest helps us.'
of course, thats no reason for complaisance - but if they were to be 
helped, narmada's waters could have been brought up some feet to their 
villages in these so many years of independance. that would have assured 
them of more than just one crop during a year. now it is limited to
that because there is no irrigation, and the crop goes by the monsoon.
that was not done in 40 odd years, today they are being vacated so that
this river's waters could be harnessed many miles away and then again 
pumped back many more miles to other more powerful people. in all these 
years of independance, there has been no road built there. how important
roads are, per se, is a moot point. but today they are being constructed 
very fast - to help evacuate these people. 

these people for most part need to be helped in their own surroundings. 
they have cattle, they have land. their mud huts are spacious and well 
built. they are poor, and live at subsistance level, but that has to 
be tackled with sympathy and understanding. 

and then when we decide to 'develop' tribals, are we ourselves 'developed' 
enough ? in our cities we are not able to manage sewage, water, pollution. 
and even things like reaching 'medical care' to them - it had to be done 
in a very understanding fashion. i hadn't realized this till one activist 
there, bela, pointed out that fact to me. after all they have their own 
system of medicine from the jungle - any introduction of new medicines 
needs to study and supplement this only. of course, vaccines may be 
needed there. and maybe other things too. but just introducing the entire 
range of allopathy there in the form a medical van may not be the solution. 

at vadgam - the first village due to get submerged - into the first hut
thats due to get submerged, the wife of chimanbhai patel, the gujarat
CM, came one evening and offered him 3 lakh rupees to move over to the 
rehabitilation site. the idea was that getting him to move would have 
would have broken the resistance in that village. and weakened the 
andolan. that simple man said that all village decisions were made in 
the open - and that if she made the same offer the next day in front 
of the village, he could accept it. she never came again. and today the 
people in the village are so proud of him. and he sits very placidly on 
his haunches, without a regret.

at gujarat the canals network is displacing many many villages. and 
today the andolan has made inroads there. the stand here is not so 
much anti dam as it is for fairer resettlement. tho' they stand to lose 
the same as the submergence villages in maharashtra/ MP, the gujarat 
government is giving them much lower compensation. and again, the 
villages on the branch canals are getting even lesser. there is one 
village, koliari, which is representative of the struggle. much before 
the NBA arrived, this village refused to move. the canal has been dug 
till this village, and then the government, not able to evict them, has 
continued digging on the other side. so this stands in the middle of the 
canal. and the villages from 1st point to 40th point ( 1st point is 
where the main canal begins, and 40th point is the point till where it 
has been dug so far. ) had come for an andolan meeting at koliari, and 
they told this village sarpanch - " you are the centre point of our 
struggle.  never give in. ". and the sarpanch later told us, " if ever 
any tribal in any part of the country is violated against, tell us.
we will immediately come to his help, leaving whatever we are doing."
and these people are simple and say just what they think. and mean
what they say.

and the gujarat govt is very short on funds right now. so the canal
work has almost stopped. and as regards the myth of the dam 
providing work to the locals, all the coolies were brought from
other states, many of them malayalees. the point being that local
people may team up and demand fair wages. so the contractor, 
jayaprakash, who had the canal construction given to him, made
this move. and these coolies haven't been paid for some months now.
the contractor says that the government hasn't been paying him. and 
the coolies, in a strange place, without money, make little trouble. 
with their money caught up, they don't even go away. tomorrow
these sardar sarovar oustees may need to go to build a dam in kerala !

again, the canal construction seems to be such that the maximum
cost is being borne by the people on one side of it, and the gain
of the water accrues to those on the other side. as it stands, the 
loss is being borne by the poorer, and the gains reach the richer.
and then there are cases of people, whose land has been divided into 
two parts by the canal who will not compensated ! and then many cases 
of the people having their land on one side of the canal, and the 
house on the other. so whereas earlier they used to run across, 
today they need to walk miles in a roundabout route over the 
nearest bridge. the engineers go to help in the drainage in the 
farms caused by the canal. there was this farmer, who kept telling 
the engineer that in all these years he has seen that the water 
drains in a certain direction in these farms. but the engineer in his 
blueprints, had the drain marked on the opposite side and would not 
listen. and today the farmer wrings his hand, the water just will not 
drain thro' the engineer's canals.

and, for some sick humour, the gujarat government's desperate move
to get funds for the dam. the riot victims in gujarat (in the aftermath
of the communal riots of december) were given some monetary aid. of this 
40% is given in the form of sardar sarovar bonds, to be encashed after 
only after 20 years. snatching from the mouths of widows, orphans, ...

along kutch, saurashtra in gujarat, this anti-dam mood is building up, 
and medha said that if they had a few more months, momentum would
really get built up. despite the strong government propoganda,
people are beginning to have doubts. and then the andolan 
inspires some respect and there is always the sentiment that, 'sarkar
tho chor hai'. { the government is a thief }

the fight is proceeding on various fronts. b.d. sharma
is a retired IAS officer - commissionar of the SC/ST - who in his
time has filed a lot of suits against the govt and is today
wholly with this movement. and then girishbahi ( girish panday) is
another elderly lawyer who is handling a lot of court cases
in this matter. but these court caes go on and on with
repeated hearings. some go in favour of the plaintiff, some
in favour of the govt. and again a lot of time and manpower gets 
spent on this - some activist usually needs to go along to the
court in most cases. 

the alternative plans for irrigation are being worked on. various 
organizations are involved in this in various ways. the NBA is also
involved in the study, withe the study being based at ahmedabad.
in ahmedabad. i wonder how much and to what extent this can be 
done. after all, the plan of the sardar sarovar must have been 
drawn by a battery of engineers working over years. to get a fraction 
of that kind of technical manpower to work on the design of
alternatives will be very difficult. not many civil engineers/
architects may be ready to give up their jobs for an attempt
like this which pays a pittance of what they could normally
earn. so finally maybe what can be acheived will be an outline of 
what can be done/ examples of what has been achieved on these lines 
on a smaller scale/ and then to mount pressure on the government to 
work in depth on these issues. for that matter, there was a file 
in the baroda office on work on alternatives. one nadkarni, who was 
a civil engineer in the gujarat government, had attacked the dam 
design long ago, and had referred the matter to the government a lot 
of times. these had either been ignored, or at times a very vague 
statement had come in response saying that all these facts were 
appreciated but ' a tribunal had been set up for the three states 
which had studied all these questions in great detail, and after 
that alone had the decisions been taken today.' nandini, before 
joining the NBA, had worked with implementing water management schemes 
in gujarat. she said that a lot of viable examples are there today, 
and the government needs to be persuaded to envision those in a bigger 
setting. 

right now, through the whole of this month, a padyatra is on thro' 
all the villages in the area. on january 30th, there was a meeting at
manibeli from where it started. a lot of supporters from other
places had joined. there were people from ekalavya, from support
groups in bombay/ delhi. during the padyatra, the andolan is finding
out about how many villages actually want to fight to the end. the 
slogan is 'hum nahin hatenge, bandh nahin banega.' but even asking them 
for a decision feels so terrible. what can they choose ? to risk all ? 
or to at least accept the pittance being offered ? after all, they are 
the ones who will actually pay the price. and why have they been pushed 
to such a point of decision ? who had the right to push them ? 

there are people in vadgam and other villages who have returned
back after having accepted the government terms and having gone to the
resettlement sites. Now they say that they'd rather drown than live 
on in those sites. they were unable to get into the resettlment lifemode. 
the sites are terrible. these people are used to a different life mode 
with vast spaces. one of the rehabitilation villages, sanoli, near 
koliari, is set up on mostly unarable land.  and the houses are one 
roomed tin shacks in which it was hard to stand in, in the heat of the 
month of january ! these were supposed to be temporary shelters - and 
they have been in this for three years now ! these people moved out from 
anthras where the andolan was not active, so they did not get the 
solidarity for the struggle. and there are other stories of where the 
government brought the people down to inspect the sites, hoping to
charm them. and when the people rejected it and demanded to go back, 
they were unceremoniously left there ! and they had to walk back for 
three days to reach thier village. in such places where villagers have moved 
to resettlement sites and then returned, the opposition to the dam is 
phenomenal. 

and in this dam, the rehabitilation has been the best in all the 
stories since independance. the plight of oustees of the other many
dams since independance does not bear thinking about.

at the outskirts of the kevadia colony, built to house the
engineers of the dam, itself, is an instance of such oustees.
the villagers who were at the site of the present township were
hounded out with minimum compensation, and they live in 
shacks at a distance from the colony. in a much lower standard
of life than they were used to.

of course, the terms of resettlement do help some oustees. the
landless get some minimum land in the deal. there are some
villages which are not part of the andolan at all, and have
shifted. the government, for the first year gives some daily
sum of money. at least on the day that we passed by, people in
one of these resettlement villages were drunk in the evening when 
we passed on, and were in a good mood. but if the money gets spent 
in drink it may not serve much purpose.

land for the oustees is brought from the rich farmers by the
govt. the rich farmers had employed a lot of people to do the
farming. today these people stand jobless, and will drift
into joining the vast tribe of "nowhere people". this has never 
been taken into account. 

and when the oustees move into the new sites, there are tremendous 
adjustment problems. there was one place where the oustees were not 
allowed to use the local cremation ground. and had to walk many 
hundred miles to their old villages to cremate their dead ! there was
one case where an infant died, and they quietly buried the child
in the resettlement site's cremation ground. the local people 
got enraged and dug up the child. there could be all kind of
caste issues involved maybe. like, perhaps, superstitions of 
lower castes defiling higher caste land.


the funds are from the local donations ( foreign funds are not accepted.) 
- the activists get a small honorarium .and because of the funds - the 
differance between the gujarat government' s propoganda of the project 
and the andolan's is vast. the government keeps coming out with glossy 
booklets full of pictures of greenery and smiling peasants.

the andolan was setting up a people's jury, where many people, including 
some eminent persons, will question various aspects of the dam exercise. if 
they manage to get the time/ manpower to organize it as they plan, they hope 
the govt too will not be able to afford to ignore it, and will need to 
face the questions.  of course, the govt may not be able to afford 
not to ignore it. but the few people there at the valley are too hard 
pressed for time and this exercise may not get built up as it should. 
and the after may things will build up for a showdown. medha said that 
that was when people would be needed most. the monsoons will start and
the 'drown squads' will take position. last year the faucets were opened, 
and flooding was not allowed. but this year the government may need 
to take a stand. the easiest thing for the government to do will be 
to round up and arresrt the protesters. but if the protest is large, 
and the number of people who proceed to the valley in solidarity then 
is large, this may not be an option.

when i was leaving, shyamala told one of the women from bargi. 
'yeh behn teen mahine ke liye gore logon ke gaon jaa rahee hai'
[ this sister is going to the village of white people for three
months. ]



I am in agreement with some who say that some of the river valley projects
that dot the modern landscape have not been without side effects. In the 
early years of the century, development of irrigation in California and 
later the TVA became forerunners and pioneers for the rest of the world to
use as a model and follow. The Aswan and the Bhakra are two such. 99% of
Egypt lives in the Nile valley a few million acres are irrigated due to this.
A thriving textile industry has grown to flourish on the border of the 
driest desert in the world. While the stench of death, and the scavenging 
flies flourish along with terrorrists and revolutionaries in neighbouring
Sudan and Ethiopia, there is hope that runs strong in modern Egypt -a great
Nasserine legacy. There is not much of a question raised about the utility
of the Aswan in the Nile valley, or of the Colorado in Napa Valley or of
the TVA in the TVA or for that matter of the Bhakra Nangal amidst the 
Bhangra singing hard working farmers on the land of the five rivers; 
however, the benefits of such projects are questioned elsewhere, by so called
farmers in Western Goa who seem to make more money writing to Illustrated
Weekly; Western Goa has no dearth of rains as for that matter Sri Lanka
(with apropos to shankar@berkeley) as is luckily drenched. I was glad to 
hear him say "environmentalists are not against dams per se". I would like
to see him set up hand pumps in Chad or for that matter certain parts of 
Madras. 
I have to agree that the benefits of the Narmada Valley Project are unlikely
to be as great as the Bhakra Nangal. I am not well versed in the science of
or the economics of such large projects. But I am pretty sure that Claude
Alvares, Medha Patkar, Sundar Lal Bahuguna are not. The TVA seems to have 
done well for 60 years. It is however important to be extremely sympathetic
to the needs and interests of those being resettled. In some sense one has
to thank the above mentioned for it is not often that the wife of a CM would
offer a lakh of rupees to a man in a hut and he would request that she offer
it publicly. The purchase of more bonds by NRIs will definitely ensure that
the needs of those resettled are not brushed aside. Personally, atleast the
equivalent of $2000 has to be offered to each of the resettled families, and
insurances taken to safeguard against untoward incidents(God forbid).
India lacks experience in executing projects of this magnitude and will need
to purchase the best know-how the world has to offer which is expensive.
It may make sense to look carefully at the project, and watch it with an
eagle eye to ensure no kick-backs(remember Veraanum). We did choose 
environment over development in the Silent Valley and there is place for
20% of the worlds cows in one of the most populated lands of the world,
all protected for religious reasons. The Krishna project which NTR started
has since stalled - and the Tehri Dam is again in controversy. I would 
agree that the Tehri Dam is much more in the grey area(probably Nuclear or
Thermal power would be better there) given that UP does not have
that much of a drought problem. 
I agree that one cannot trust the govt. to handle the issue properly. I 
would therefore suggest that a separate fund be started to give money to 
those being resettled through without entrusting this job to the govt.
This is best done either by collecting the money and giving it to Medha 
Patkar or through some other private agency which would dispense it without
leakage and to ensure that those displaced do not get a raw deal. As the govt,.is likely to go on with the project anyway(as it has indicated) it is best to
do it well.

rats before working Americans and Wrong Albert, The central organizing 
principle of this nation is freedom". Just kidding, I am not a Republican.
