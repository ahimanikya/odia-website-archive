                  ALTERNATIVES TO NARMADA DAM

      THE CASE FOR SUSTAINABLE PROSPERITY  by   PRAFUL BIDWAI

      The Times of India, August 11, 1994 (Bombay Edition)


                    When Mr. T.N.Seshan,then secretary,environment,gave
conditional clearence to the sardar sarovar dam project inJune 1987, he
could have scarcely imagined that each one of the conditions would
repetedly and calculatedly be violated as the authorities in Gujarat and
Maharashtra proceeded to build the reservoir, devastating the lives of
thousands of vulnerable people. Nor could the supreme Court judges,who
stipulated an important condition on rehabilitation four years on, have
believed that that too would be violated .

The government of Gujarat and the Sardar Sarovar Nigam have engaged in a
charade of seeking sanction and legitimacy for their project.The story of
the  sarovar is nothing if not a tale of wilful breach by the authorities
of crucial stipulations of the original awards of the Narmada tribunal,
Numerous conditions laid down by the Narmada Control Authority, and court
injunctions. The sponsors of the project have shown utter contempt for
sound planning environmental protection and the rehabilitation of the oustees.

PM's INTERVENTION

since November 1993 alone, the authorities have committed no fewer than22
such violations.Even while they were being given clearence in January to
build the dam upto the height of 61 metres due to the Prime Minister's own
Intervention , they had already built it six metres higher. By March, they
had proceede to close the sluice gates without the environment
department's permission. And now they have raised the dam to a height of
106 metres--- without sanction warrant or authority. The dam authorities
seem bent on demonstrating that for them might is right as they proceed to
submerge entire tribal villages.The historic temple of Shulpaneshwar in
Manibeli already stands under 20 mt of water.As the stranded tribals,
without  land or shelter, await their fate-- arrest and forcible
eviction,or death by drowning --the time has come to take a hard look at
the SS project.

               The project bristles with problems. Its economics is unsound.
Its capital cost --originally estimated at Rs 13,640 crore and certain to
escalate, as wellas irrigation mega-projects is so high that it will take
over Rs one lakh to irrigate a hectare. The annualised cost exceeds half
the value of agricultural output per hectare. The project is
environmentally unviable, as the review commission by World Bank discovered.
None of the studies which Mr Seshan insisted be completed by 1989 to draw
up remedial action plans has yet been done. The rehabilitation problems
involved are almost intractable : displacement of about two lakh people, 
half of them tribals; scarcity of land on which to resettle them in
conformity with the Tribunal award; absence of measures to repair and
restore ruptured communities with a uniquely symbiotic relationship with
nature.

       Behind the authorities' condemnation of their critics and their
disdain for legalities lies besides the raw power of kulaks and building
contractors, an overweening technological arrogance and the assumption
that there is no alternative to the SS project as formulated today. This
assumption is wrong. There are perfectly rational, technically sound,
environmentally superior, cost-effective and humane alternatives. The
Jayant Patil independent review group   appointed by the centre
--and prevented even from visiting Gujarat -- has alone received more than
 ten proposals from such sources as two former chief irrigation engineers
of Madhya Pradesh, a highly regarded energy expert(Mr AKN Reddy),an
appropriate technology specialist (Mr K R Datye), geologists, ecologists
and other scientists. While some call for scrapping the SS dam altogether  
and taking different approach to water resource use, others favour a
reduction of its height, and its power output, while retaining the
irrigation potential. This last suggestion has been made by no less than the
MP chief minister who has offered to make good the loss of power to
Gujarat on height reduction from 455 to 436 feet, which would reduce the
displacement and submergence by 26 and 29 percent respectively.

AREA INCREASED

The latest, and in some ways most remarkable, alternative has been
proposed by an engineer and social scientist, Mr Suhas Paranjape,with Mr K J
Joy. The PJ plan entails no compromise on Gujarat's share of water or power.
It increases the irrigation source area from 1.8 mha to 4.15 mha. But it
involves new priorities of water use, redistribution of water flows within
Gujarat, emphasis on maximising biomass production and generating other
economic activities. The plan will lower the dam height to 300 feet, but
increase the arid Kutch's share of water fivefold to 10 percent, and that
of water-starved Saurashtra and north Gujarat from 22 and 17 percent to 32
and 36 percent. This means creating two systems of barrages and
integrating the canal system with groundwater and surface storages. The
whole plan will cost ,along with supplementary electrical cogeneration
(solar r gas) Rs 11,670 crore or lessthan the existing SS plan.

      The real superiority of the PJ plan is not so much economic as
social, environm,ental and developmental. It will reduce submergence
by over 70 percent and the number of affected villages by 80 to 90
percent. This will not only help save over 10,000 hectares of forest,
but minimise the breakup of tribal communities.It will also make
environmental problems such as salinity and backwater effect more
manageable. More important , it will allocate water more equitably,
both regionally and by giving priority to to meet basic needs, and
making this conditional upon an environmentally sound land use
pattern: mix of foodgrains, agro-forestry and other crops.  No less
significant is the emphasis on local materials and resources, use of
existing surface storages, and decentralized water use through user's
cooperative groups. Locally, the whole system will be centered on
storages, not canals, and will thus limit state interference.

PLAN INTEGRATED


The water plan is integrated into a larger scheme for regenerative
agriculture, building of the infrastructure and a construction
industry using local materials, and the generation of energy through
innovative mix of sources : biofuel,solar,wind, hydel. The plan seeks
to provide enough water to generate 18 tons of biomass per family,
enough for its food,fuel, fodder and cash income needs.
    The potential for biomass production is large enough for over 2000
MW of power generation.This leads to a larger plan of self-reliance
and sustainable prosperity. The cost of power is as low as Rs 1.20 to
Rs.1.60 per unit for 40 KW and two MW workplaces. If 2.1 million
families can be organized around a total of about 18,000 such
workplaces, each could reasonably lookforward to an additional income
of Rs. 18,000 a year. What does this mean in real terms? Potentially,
such a plan would enable the average person in the Narmada valley to
consume of the energy equivalent of about 3,000 kg of coal. This is
the same level as the per capita energy consumption in pre- World War
Two Europe, barring space heating and urban transportation. This
willtake the people of the Narmada valley to an altogether
qualitatively higher level of prosperity on an equitable , humane,
ecologically sound and sustainable basis.

       The vision underlying the PJ plan is grand, but it is also
based on some hard numbers. One of its great merits is that it cuts
such Gordian knots as big vs. small dams, less power or more water,
and the displacement calculus. The plan , like several other options,
demands serious and dispassionate discussion.
