	SOME INFO on the microirrigation method called Drip-irrigation
			--------------------------------

  
The term "microirrigation" is used to describe irrigation systems which use
low flow rate emitting devices (emitters) to place water on the soil surface
very near the plants being irrigated or below the surface directly into the
plant root zone. Microirrigation systems are extensively used in Florida,
both in commercial agriculture as well as in landscape irrigation. 

Microirrigation systems are characterized by the use of small diameter,
flexible polyethylene lateral pipes, low flow rates per emitter, and
operation at low pressures. Normally only a portion of the crop root zone
is irrigated. Frequent, small applications are required to keep the water
content of the irrigated root zone near field capacity. Smaller volumes of
water are typically applied with microirrigation systems as compared to
conventional irrigation systems, thus the term "low volume irrigation
system" is sometimes used to describe a microirrigation system. 

Specific types of microirrigation systems include drip (or trickle),
microsprinkler (or microspray), line source, and bubbler systems. 

Chemicals are applied through most microirrigation systems to enhance
crop growth and to prevent emitter clogging. Fertilizers can be efficiently
applied with the irrigation water to meet the plant needs throughout the
growing season. Water conditioning and cleaning agents are normally
required as a part of a regular maintenance program to prevent clogging
of emitters due to chemical precipitation or biological growths. 
Fertigation and other chemigation activities involve the application of
chemicals (fertilizer or other) into the irrigation system and use
the irrigation system to convey the chemical to the crop.
Because microirrigation systems can place water with high levels of uniformity
in the crop root zone, they are particularly adapted to chemigation.
Fertigation can be used to provide nutrients to the crop as needed. This
can improve the overall fertilizer application efficiency and reduce the
amount of nutrients leached out of the crops root zone.

Well-designed and well-managed microirrigation systems are very
efficient in terms of both water and energy use. Micro systems have been
shown to increase crop yields and decrease nutrient losses from leaching
because they permit prescription applications of water and fertilizers to be
made. Water with higher salinity levels than required by other methods
can often be used because micro systems maintain high soil water contents
with frequent irrigations, they can be designed to avoid wetting the plant
foliage, and salt and water movement is downward away from the plant
roots. 

Disadvantages

The small orifices in microirrigation emitters are easily clogged by
particulate matter, chemical precipitates, and biological growths. Thus,
microirrigation requires that systems be regularly flushed and that water
be filtered and chemically treated to reduce clogging problems. Some
water sources may not be suitable for microirrigation because filtration
and chemical treatment costs would be excessive. 

Microirrigation systems normally have greater maintenance requirements
than sprinkler systems. There are usually many more components, because
each emitter covers a much smaller area than a sprinkler. Also, emitters
are typically plastic and much more susceptible to wear and breakage than
sprinklers made from metal alloys. 
The cost of microirrigation systems can be very high with respect to
conventional systems for closely spaced plants or crop rows. For example,
the initial cost of a microirrigation system is much lower than the cost of a
sprinkler system for widely-spaced crops such as citrus, while the initial
cost of microirrigation for some types of ornamental nursery production
systems is $40,000 per acre. This is approximately 20 times the cost of a
sprinkler irrigation system for the same area.

Sorry for not getting back to the group earlier.

I was waiting to be able to complete the drip irrigation
system in my yard before writing about it.

Here is my experience so far:

It took me 5 hours to complete the system
which involved 200 ft of main line (i.e. tubes of half inch width)
and another 200 ft of branch lines (i.e. tubes of quarter inch
width). The system will water 30 plants (8 trees of size 10 ft,
and rest are medium size planst such as rose bush).

In my opinion installing was easy. 
But I got all the components from Home Depot.
Considering that India Today said farmers in western India
have started using this method, I assume the different
parts of the system are avialable now in India. But how easily
I don't know. 


For my system, I connected a  1/2 inch tube to the water outlet
for the garden hose. I then using T sections, right angle sections,
and connectors made a main line around the house. 
Branch lines (1/4 inch tubes) were connected to the main line 
by making a small hole (tool for making hole bought from
Home Depot) on the main line and then using connectors that 
went into that hole on one end and to the 1/4 inch tube
in the other end. Drippers (bought from Home depot) were then 
put on the other end of the 1/4 tube. Drippers were then put near the root
of the plants.  Drippers with different ratings (1 gallon per hour etc)
are available in Home dept and depending on the plan size 
I put appropriate drippers.
Thats the basic process. The parts were designed very well
in the sense that only tool I needed was a tool to make hole
on the tube and something to cut the pipes.
(There were no threading except when the tube is connected to
the hose outlet.)


For the drip system to work it needs to be supplied with
water at a certain pressure. It is easy to ensure in homes
in USA. In India I don't know how to do that. My  guess
would be we would need some kind of a pump. 
On the other hand we might need to consider pumps to
get water from wells. Especially in the dry part of Orissa
getting water from the under ground is probably the only 
choice. Here (in US) the underground is mapped and 
it is known how much water is undergraound in what place.
I assume such geological map is available in Orissa and
hopefully there is enough water underground in the targeted 
areas.


Hope this helps.
Chitta

The following reply is from Dr. James Ayars, research leader of Water 
Management Research Lab. He answers the questions on drip-irrigation,
and on using fly-ash fertilizers:

Dear Mr. Patra:  Drip irrigation is very good way of applying small amounts
of water very precisely.  However, it is also a very expensive technology.
The drip tubing and emitters can be very expensive to purchase depending on
the type selected.  It also requires very high levels of filtration to
ensure that the emitters don't get plugged.  You also have to have a pumping
plant to pressurize the system and pressure regulators to control the flow
and amount applied.  A standard estimate of cost in the U.S. would be
approximately $1000 to $1500 per acre.  Drip irrigation also requires very
careful management.  Poor quality water can also plug the drip emitters.
There is a book by Bucks and Nakayama which describes drip/trickle
irrigation very well.  You might try to find it and use it as a study guide.
There is a discussion group on trickle irrigation called TRICKLE-L which you
might want to join and ask if anybody has any experience with drip
irrigation in developing countries which does not require the technology you
encounter in the developed countries.  The use os sewage sludge and fly ash
is worth considering. You would have to surface apply it and then irrigate.
You would not be able to apply that material with a drip system.  The only
concern with using those materials would be the presence of heavy metals
which could contaminate the soils eventually.  I hope this gets you started
in your searches.  We have several publications on drip/trickle irrigation
available at the Water Management Research Laboratory.  The list is
published on our home page http://asset.arsusda.gov/WMRL.html.  You can
check the list and request the publications. We will need a return surface
mail address and the number of the publication you would like to receive.
Please feel free to contact me if I can be of any further help.  Sincerely
James E. Ayars
