A glimpse of Orissa in folksy harmony
                ---------------------------------------
                        by Priyadarsan Patra


	The State of Orissa is located in the east coast of India, bounded
by the States of Andhra Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh, Bihar and West
Bengal.  Broadly the territory of the State May be divided into four
distinct regions. (1) The Eastern plateau (the districts Of
Mayurbhanj, Keonjhar, Sundargarh and the Palalahara Subdivision of
Dhenkanal district) is an undulating upland frequently intersected by
hill ranges and generally slopes down from north to south. (2) The
Central River Basin (the districts of Balangir, Sambalpur and
Dhenkanal) is washed by many major rivers of the State and contains
some most fertile parts of the State. (3) The Eastern Hill Region to
the south and south-west of the Central River Basin (erstwhile
districts of Phulbani, Ganjam and Koraput) comprise of wide open
upland plateau fringed by forests. (4) The Coastal Belt (the districts
of Balasore, Cuttack, Puri and a portion of Ganjam) has extensive
alluvial tract in between the hills of the west and the salty tracts
of the east.  Orissa, with her 156,000 sq. kms. of land, 13 percent of
which is urban, is home to nearly 32 million people. Literacy rate
grazes below the national average of 52%.

	Through the centuries, Orissa has been a meeting place of various
cultural migrations from different parts of the country. The economic
life of the people of Orissa centers mainly around agriculture where
one out of every four persons in Orissa is a tribal.  At the same
time, the State is on the threshhold of an industrial growth with
emphasis on ferro alloys, electronics, iron and mine industries, and
production of cash crops like tea, coffee, rubber, cashew nuts,
spices, etc.

History of Orissa 
   
 
	Several kingdoms, viz, Kalinga, Utkala, Odra, Kosala, Tosali and
Kangoda, had variously flourished in the region currently known as
Orissa. Orissa, pronounced Odissa, comes from the Sanskrit 'Odra
Desha' or 'Odra Visha'. The words 'Odra' and 'Utkala' are thought to
be the Aryan versions of 'Okkal' and 'Oddisu' in some Dravidian
languages meaning the cultivator. After all, the culture of Orissa is
a hoary mixture of the Savara, the Dravidian and the Aryan cultures.
Some experts argue that Utkala, another name for Orissa appearing in
the Indian national anthem "Jana Gana Mana...," stands for "Kala re
Utkarsa" in Oriya, or "excellence in art". Although the recorded
history of Orissa dates back to her gory conquest by Emperor Ashoka in
260 B.C. (which lead a remorseful Ashoka to adopt non-violence), and
later to the glorious times of Kalinga Emperor Kharavela, the kingdoms
of Kalinga, Utkala, Odra and Kosala are said to have politically
united forming Orissa under the stewardship of Maharaja Anantavarman
Chodagangadev (1078-1147 A.D.).

Ganga dynasty coming on the heels of Somavamsis, who built the
magnificent Lingaraj Temple at Bhubaneswar, in the late 11th century
produced some of the greatest conquerers and builders in history of
Orissa.  The sacred Hindu temple of Lord Jagannath built during
Anantavarman's time had attracted the eminent Vaishnava scholar and
reformist Ramanuja. Legend has it that after the Mahabharata War, Sri
Krishna was accidentally shot by tribesman Jara Sabar and later
creamated by the Pandava brothers at Koili Baikuntha, now within the
Jagannath Temple. The legend further unfolds into a lilting story of how
king Indradyumna's messenger Vidyapati fell in love with the daughter
of tribal Chief Viswabasu who had discovered the indestructible heart
of Krishna in the sea and had been secretly worshipping It
since. Later, by Krishna's grace, gods' architect Vishwakarma built a
temple for the sacred deity known as Jagganath of Puri -- which was to
become a seat of Sanskrit scholarship.


  King Narasingha Deva (1238-1264 A.D.)  built the world-famous
architectural marvel called the Sun temple at Konark or the "Black
Pagoda". There are many legends associated with this Temple, a most
moving of which tells the story of a 12-year old boy Dharama only who
was able to install the Temple finial and yet jumped down to his death to
save the lives of his father and 1200 assistants, in charge of temple
construction, who Dharama thought would be beheaded by the king if he
learns the architects failed again to complete the Temple by
themselves.

 
	Gajapati ruler Kapilendra Deb (1436- A.D.) was a brilliant military
strategist who defeated the Bengal Sultan and Vijayanagar king to
expand his empire from the Ganges in the north to Kavery in the
south. After the fall of the Suryavamsi kings ruling over a
multi-lingual Orissa, she suffered a serious and swift political, and
economic decline during the period 1566-1803 which culminated in
British conquering and dividing her into three pieces and attaching
them to the presidencies of Bengal, Madras and the Central Provinces.
The first gallant, mass-based revolt against the British, known as
"Paika Bidroha", of 1817 was quelled ruthlessly. Neglect of the
region's infrastructure and agriculture by the East India Company was
so total that the famine of 1866 A.D., the legendary Naanka Durbhikhya
(nine-years of drought), wiped out nearly one-third of the State's
population!  A nationalist movement among Oriya-speaking people led
to the creation of separate Orissa province in 1936.

	The maritime history of Orissa reached its pinnacle during the
reign of Sailodbhaba kings of Kangoda (areas around lake Chilika and
Ganjam district). They carried with them Indian art, architecture,
trade and culture, and established the Shailendra empire (as colonies)
over islandic countries such as Indonesia, Borneo, Sumatra, Java and
Malayasia often known as 'Suvarna Dwipa.'
  
	Jainism, Budhism, Saivism, vaishnavism -- all have found patronage
of the Orissan rulers at different times as evident in the Budhist
monuments, Jainist cave writings, Saivite Lingaraj Mandir, and
Vaishnavite Jagannath Temple, etc.


The "Girijan" or Tribals

	Orissa is one of the States in Eastern India which can boast of a
vast tribal population belonging to diverse races and culture.  In the
hilly tracts of the Western Orissa, there is heavy concentration of
tribal population and in the coastal districts they constitute a low
percentage.  The majority of this population inhabits the forests and
hilly regions of Mayurbhanj, Keonjhar, Phulbani, Sundargarh, Balangir,
Kalahaiidi and Koraput districts. Orissa has as many as 62 tribes,
many of the important ones are Kondh, Koya, Bonda, Gadaba, Paraja,
Santal, Juang, Saora, Gond, Kharia, Bhuyan etc.  Many of the
tribes have a distinct language and culture of their own and their
socioeconomic pattern of living varies from tribe to tribe and region
to region. Their economic life centers round pursuits from hunting and
food gathering to shifting and settled cultivation.  Although the
tribals have retained at many places their traditional way of life,
many things are changing fast under the impact of modern civilisation
and various development programmes.
 
Arts and Crafts 

	Famous for the beauty and craftsmanship of Konark, Jagannath,
Lingaraj and innumerable other temples, Orissa also offers a striking
selection of decorative and utilitarian arts and crafts. Visual
manifestations of a millenium of Orissan culture, these arts and
crafts embody a tradition which still lives in the creative
imagination and skill of her craftsmen. The applique works of Pipili,
Patachitras and Papier Mache masks of Puri, Silver Filigree jewellery
and golden grass mats of Cuttack, Sambulpuri sarees, Dhokra and clay
toys of Mayurbhanj, lacquer work of Nawrangpur, horn work of
Parlakhemundi or the flexible brass fish of Ganjam-- each handicraft
exudes an originality of its own.

	The culture of Orissa has been deeply influenced by its tribal
population and one of the most important aspect of tribal culture is
music and dance. Among the Juang and Santal, for example, young men
cannot find brides unless they acquire special skill in dance and
music.  Among tribal and peasant populations alike, dance and music
are primarily meant to propitiate Gods and Goddesses. In social
functions like marriage and feasting, dance is their usual hobby.  The
dance is performed in a central place of the village or in the
communal house and attended by almost all the tribesmen. Each tribe
has its own pattern of dance and music. Dances such as Chhau and
Paika, musicals like Pala and Daskathia, rhythmic instruments such
as Dhola, Madala and Jhanja, and the folk renditions such as epic
Sarala Mahabharata all have a distinctive Oriya folk signature.
	
Danda Nata is a month-long theatrical and religious festival depicting
the tribal way of welcoming the New Year beginning with the month of
Vaishakh. The participants of the ritualized performances remain
celibate and abstemious, consider social reform besides believing that
the celebration will fulfil their heart's desires.  The theme of Danda
Nata is at times taken from Sanskrit literature, yet the style of
performance has a tribal and folk accent. It took shape as performing
art with ritual overtones during 9th-lOth century and over time it has
embraced non-tribals as well.

Odissi, a lyrical form of both dance and music, with its own
intricacies of rhythm, mime, and poetic posture ("Bhangi" and
"Lasya"), has roots in the devotional tradition of Lord Jagannath, a
revered god of tribals too.

Festivals 

Oriyas celebrate many festivals, rich and colorful and often
associated with enchanting legends.  The most famous one
is Ratha Jatra or Car Festival at Puri.  The sacred journey of Lord
Jagannath on richly caparisoned and decorated chariot to Mausima
Mandira for 9 days' stay marks the occasion.

The famous spring festival Dola Purnima (Holi) is a five days long
celebration in which images of Radha and Krishna are taken out in
procession. The last day of these is observed by throwing colored
water on one another and smearing each other's face with colored
powder.

Danda in Danda Jatra means a stick as well as punishment.  The
"Bhoktas" or participants of Dhooli, Pani, Agni and Bana Danda show
sheer acrobatics and intense endurance: in Agni Danda, the Bhoktas
walk on burning charcoal and thorns. Tribal and folk themes, and
episodes from the Ramayana and Shiva Purana are enacted through
rituals, dances, songs and comic actions. The celebrations performed
each day of the festival peak on the last day, Meru Sankranti, with
much improvisation and pageantry.

 
Dipavali, Boita Bandana (commemorating past mercantile voyages of
Oriyas), Jagar Jatra, Sital Sasthi, Ashokastami, Janmasthami, Kuanra
Purnima, Raja, and Akhaya Trutiya (marking the season of paddy
sowing) are some of the other popular festivals.

  Sandwiched between north and south India, Orissa is an old land with
an ancient heritage.  The daily living, food, costume, the
narrativeness, the legends, the musicality and the dance steps reflect
the life of a people deeply committed to independence and artistic
excellence.
