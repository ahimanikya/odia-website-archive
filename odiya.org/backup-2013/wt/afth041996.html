Dear Editor,

It was a great pleasure to go through the Weekend Thoughts this time. Dr. Sahu's article is definitely a laudable effort at analysing the caste system. I would, however, like to mention some points which would only act as an 'add-on'. I would like to discuss some functions that the caste system performed in the past which perhaps explain its ubiquitous nature and, of course, the drawbacks of this ascriptive system of stratification and also about the relevance of caste in contemporary India.

As Dr. Sahu rightly points out, the caste system is unique to Hinduism. Though every society has some sort of stratification based on the division of labour, what makes caste system special is the fact that the gradation of occupations is based on the concept of purity and pollution i.e. it has a religious sanction which confers some legitimacy to the system.

Every society graduates from a stage of mechanical solidarity (where integration of the society springs from the fact that all the members perform basically the same task ) to a stage of organic solidarity ( where the indivi- duals have independent and specialised functions but are interdependent on each other for the maintenance of the social system as a whole ). Caste system is said to symbolise this organic solidarity which was conducive to social cohe- sion and stability. It was oriented towards welfare of all. Though hierarchical, the caste system was cooperative nonetheless.From the individual point of view, the system provided him from birth with a fixed social millieu. He's provided in this way with a permanent body of associations which canalised his contacts and his choice in marriage and, if need be, provided for his funeral. Earlier, the caste system was basically an extended kin-group. It roughly determined the social status of each caste in relation to other castes.It passed down the pattern of skills and knowledge. Craft secrets were well safeguarded and perpetuated by occupational castes. In this way, the system acted as a guild or trade union. It performed economic functions as well in the form of Jajmani relationships. Apart from that, the caste system acted as a political stabiliser which is probably the reason that India survived in the face of vast number of invasions including some which were essentially antagonistic to Hinduism.It even survived conversion to other religions. In a nutshell, the caste system provided a social structure which organised the political, economic and ritual life of the people of India.

Nonetheless,the caste system of the past suffered from serious drawbacks. First of all, though cooperative, the caste system was not egalitarian running counter to the belief in 'equal opportunity for all'. The hierarchy was not altogether altruistic. It was basically exploitation by a highly intelligent group under the cover of a religious philosophy too subtle for the masses. it was effectively utilised by the upper castes to maintain the existing form as it worked to their advantage. The system offered very little mobility. The occupational inheritance prevented innovation. It prevented the Indian society from having a national life by splitting it into caste groups. The caste system also had dele- terious effect on the status of women through the custom of sati, ban on widow remarriage and the like. It also posed an obstacle to modern capitalism and industry.

Today, the traditional caste system as a set of occupationally-specialised, interdependent castes ranked by purity and pollution customs has shown signs of fading away. The rules regarding commensality and segregration have lost their relevance in the present context.But it still remains a vital element of Indian society as far as its endogamous nature is concerned. Mandalisation has made Indians more caste conscious. In the past, there was not much protest about caste oppression as the lower castes thought it to be the divine will. They were fatalistic in their outlook. The tables have turned because now the dominant castes question the relevance of caste and its the depressed castes who want to hang onto their caste-status as it works out to their advantage. There is definitely the need to make up for the oppression inflicted on the lower castes in the past. But there has been insignificant improvement in the conditions of these masses even after almost fifty years of reservation and its the creme layer of the low castes who avail all the benefits. So the reservation policy is not likely to be tampered with atleast for another couple of decades. This will continue to ingrain casteism and caste antagonism in the coming generation which will become even more hostile to the fact of weightage being given to ascription rather than meritocracy and achievment. As Dr. Sahu points out, elections are fought on the basis of caste to take advantage of the illiteracy of the Indian voters. Thus as long as caste is used as a political tool to manipulate power, it continues.

To think of a casteless homogeneous Indian society is perhaps to dwell in utopian idealism. But only when we have dreams that we strive to achieve them. The first step probably would be to ignore all those elements that that give prominence to caste because anything, either in individual capacity or in administration, that has some reference to caste creates tender feelings about it. The state will have a big role to play in this. For instance, instead of making caste the criterion for deciding the downtrodden sections, it can adopt the economic criterion ( without any referenct caste whatsoever ) for positive discrimination. Inter caste marriage will go a long way towards the realisation of this dream. Caste fusion would eliminate caste-patriotism. But as long as marriage is an affair of the parents, it is going to be difficult as parents would like to go for a person having similar background so that the marriage is compatible. More often than not, the background they look for is caste. So the youth needs to take charge of matrimonial affairs. For this to take place, co-education is very important to give proper opportunities to come in contact with the opposite sex. The enthusiasm of the youth will then transcend the artificial boundaries of caste. Moreover education at the grassroot level will definitely help in creating open-minded Indians. But education only is not sufficient. It has to be accompanied by employment opportunities. I remember when my class had gone on a field trip to Anand in Gujarat, we had come across a bunch of educated, but unemployed youths who sounded really passionate about the caste issue. Now the die-hard spirit is good psychical energy but only if channelised properly. I wished they could use this enthusiasm elsewhere effectively ! So, India's development would likely be accompanied by the decline in the involvement in casteism.

Now, it can be argued that that there is no harm in being proud of one's caste when one can be proud of being an Oriya, an Indian and the like. But they are not analogous so far as caste is seen as an institution which has no concept of dignity of labour, which entailed exploitation of the masses and worse still, perpetrated their low condition.

Last but not the least, I would like to add another analogous form of discrimination apart from caste and race discrimination cited by Dr.Sahu - gender discrimination. All three operate along the same line - inferiority/ superiority by birth. In fact, gender discrimination is most ascriptive of all as caste status can change ( Sanskritisation ), colour can change (miscegenetion) but not sex. Well, ... !

I would like to sign off by saying that it would be nice to indulge in positive thinking - be a humanist rather than a racist, casteist or sexist.And as far as individual contribution is concerned, we could start off by sponsoring a child. It will be only a drop in the ocean, but at least a drop !

DEEPA PARIJA
New Orleans

One of the masterpiece review I have ever come across in this forum.

Few comments on the topic and Deepa's review:

Mr. Sahu although did a good job, his perspective on historicity of Varna and Caste system is not well founded. I may write on this provided I see some comments on the topic from some members of the higher caste, particularly Brahmins.

The review by Deepa is founded on actual facts and truth. She seems to be well versed with the social system prevailing in India. She has properly done her homework before she picked up the subject of her review. But I don't agree with her on two issues.

...They were fatalistic in their outlook. The tables have turned because now the dominant castes question the relevance of caste and its the depressed castes who want to hang onto their caste-status as it works out to their advantage...

I doubt that the dominant castes question the relevance of caste. In fact it is other way around. Higher castes have been subconsciously perpetuating caste system in their subconscious attempt at maintaining the status quo. This is exemplified during such social behavior as marriage. Most of my Brhamin friends have outrightly declared that they can't even think about marrying anybody from other caste, be it because of family or social pressure. Atleast for lower caste sticking to their caste structure is understandable, for they achieve some immediate educational and economical advantage because of the present constitutional set up. To have a clear idea about how it works one must refer to the work by great Indian sociologist M. N. Srinivas. Dr. Yogevendra Singh has also thrown considerable light on these social equations. I think higher castes in their subconscious attempt at maitaining their past social and political hegemony and lower castes in their attempt at achieving immediate economic and political gains have helped perpetuate caste system in modern India.

In Orissa contest, I feel Brhamins are most casteist, and Karanas have been most forthcoming in accepting the emerging new trends in the society. In Indian contest, Bengal, Gujrat, Punjab and to some extent Orissa, Maharastra and Kerala have seen rapid changes vis-a-vis caste system, and have been a dominant force in integrating the society based on economic and educational achievement.
