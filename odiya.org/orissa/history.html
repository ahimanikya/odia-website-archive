Political history of Orissa

Several kingdoms, viz, Kalinga, Utkala, Udra/Odra, Kosala, Tosali and Kangoda, had variously flourished in the region currently known as Orissa. Orissa, pronounced Odissa, comes from the Sanskrit 'Odra Desha' or `Odra Visha'. The words 'Odra' and 'Utkala' are thought to be the Aryan versions of 'Okkal' and 'Oddisu' in some Dravidian languages meaning the cultivator. After all, the culture of Orissa is a hoary mixture of the Savara, the Dravidian and the Aryan cultures well-toasted by mutual influences and counter-influences. Some experts argue that Utkala, another name for Orissa appearing in the Indian national anthem ``Jana Gana Mana...,'' denotes Kala re Utkarsa in Oriya language; translated, it means "excellence in art". The kingdoms of Kalinga, Utkala, Odra and Kosala are said to have politically united forming Orissa under the stewardship of maharaja Anantavarman Chodagangadev (1078-1147 A.D.).

Ashoka, the Great, conquered Kalinga in 261 B.C. and yet, he himself got conquered by ``the river of blood from thousands of the brave, slain people of Orissa.'' He turned from Chandashoka into Dharmashoka in the wake of the terrible war. He renounced violence, converted to Budhism, and adopted the path of peace and welfare, as his Kalinga edict declares, ``All men are my children. Just as for my children, I desire that they be united with welfare and happiness of this world.'' Significantly, this transformation was to change later the course of history in Asia!

A couple of hundred years thence, the Kalinga empire had its hey day as an imperial power during the reign of the greatest Kalinga emperor Kharavela in the 1st century B.C. who held his sway over a considerable portion of the north, south and west India.

The glorious maritime history of Orissa reached its pinnacle during the reign of Sailodbhaba kings of Kangoda (areas around lake Chilika and Ganjam district). They carried with them Indian art, architecture, trade and culture, and established the Shailendra empire (as colonies) over islandic countries such as Indonesia, Borneo, Sumatra, Java and Malayasia often known as `Suvarna Dwipa.' The empire, called by their natives as `Kalinga,' lasted for about two centuries from the 8th century A.D. (In most parts of Orissa, people still celebrate this as `Boita Bandana' by floating paper or plantain-trunk boats with candles on Kartik Purnami day.)

Orissa has a very chequered religious history. Jainism, Budhism, Saivism, vaishnavism -- all have found patronage of the rulers at different times. (Orissa still boasts, deservingly, of religious and ethnic tolerance in the modern, turbulent political atmosphere.) One of the glorious periods of Orissan history (736-930 A.D.) saw 6 women among the 18 Bhaumakar rulers who presided over the formative period of the present-day Oriya language and culture.

The Somavamsi kings, whose capital was Jajpur town, reigned from last quarter of 9th century to end of the 11th. These followers of Shaivism built the magnificent Lingaraj temple among others.

Ganga dynasty coming on the heels of Somavamsis in the late 11th century produced some of the greatest conquerers and builders in history of Orissa. Anant Barman Chodagangadev is credited to have united all parts of Orissa and to have introduced an efficient administration. He moved his capital from modern Mukhalingam to Cuttack in 1135 A.D. The sacred Hindu temple of Lord Jagannath built during his time had attracted the eminent Vaishnava scholar Ramanuja (who introduced several reforms in the system of worship). King Narasingha Deva (1238-1264 A.D.) built the world-famous architectural marvel that the Sun temple of Konark is.

Kapilendra Deb, the first Gajapati ruler, ascended the throne of Orissa in 1436 A.D. He was a most brilliant military strategist who defeated the Bengal Sultan and Vijayanagar king to expand his empire from the Ganges in the north to Kavery in the south. After the fall of the Suryavamsi Gajapati kings ruling over a multi-lingual Orissa, it suffered a serious and swift political, territorial and economic decline during the period 1566-1803. Quickly afterwards, Orissa greatly suffered under the subjugation of the British rule. The first mass, gallant revolt against the British, known as "Paika Bidroha", of 1817 was quelled ruthlessly. Neglect of the region's infrastructure and agriculture by the East India Company was so total that the famine of 1866 A.D., the legendary Naanka Durbhikhya, wiped out nearly one-third of the state's population!

Wanton dismemberment and suffering of the Oriya-speaking areas started during the Mughal period and reached a crescendo during the British rule, severely handicapping the people of Orissa in all aspects: political, economic, administrative and cultural. Following a widespread agitation for years, a separate, linguistic province of Orissa was born on April 1, 1936. Several large, Oriya-speaking tracts such as Midnapore in Bengal, Singhbhum in Bihar, Bastar in Madhya Pradesh, and Manjusa in Andhra Pradesh remained excluded from the new province.

Orissa vigorously participated in the national movement for freedom under the able tutelage of great men and women like Utkalmani Gopabandhu Das, Utkalgaurav Madhusudan Das, and Mrs. Malti Choudhury, et al. (Salt Satyagraha movement's success in Orissa was apparently second only to that in Gujurat.) Orissa was pronounced a state upon India's independence. Later, following the merger of the often-oppressive princely states in 1948-49, Orissa was organized into 13 districts and 57 subdivisions. (Now, they are divided into 26 revenue districts.)
